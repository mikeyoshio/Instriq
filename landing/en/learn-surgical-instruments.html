How to learn surgical instruments, step by step

Don't try to memorise two hundred clamps at once. Instruments are learned by function and by comparing the ones that look alike. This guide gives you an order and a method, with a link to each instrument's entry.

For every instrument, learn four things

  1. Its name, and the other names you may hear.
  2. How to recognise it: the tip, the shanks, the ratchet, the size.
  3. What it is for: which tissue and at which stage of the operation.
  4. How it differs from the one it resembles.

The eight groups, in this order

Cutting

Scalpel (handle + blade)
Numbered handle and disposable blade; the blade is always loaded and removed with a needle holder, never with fingers.
Mayo scissors
Sturdy, with heavy blades: dense tissue and cutting sutures.
Metzenbaum scissors
Long handles and fine blades: delicate tissue. Not for cutting suture.

Dissecting

Toothed dissecting forceps
Teeth at the tip: hold skin and firm tissue without slipping.
Non-toothed dissecting forceps
Serrated tip: delicate tissue, vessels and bowel.
Adson forceps, toothed
Short, with a broad grip and a very fine tip: skin and closure.

Haemostasis

Mosquito forceps (Halstead)
The smallest, with fine tips: small vessels.
Kelly forceps
Medium size. The classic Kelly is serrated only on the distal half; the Crile, along the whole jaw (many catalogues treat them as synonyms).
Rochester-Pean forceps
Large and strong, fully serrated jaw: pedicles and thick tissue.

Grasping

Allis forceps
Small teeth at the tip: firm grip, for tissue that will be removed.
Babcock forceps
Rounded, fenestrated tip without teeth: atraumatic, for bowel and tubes.
Ring forceps (Foerster)
Ring tips: holding a swab for prepping or haemostasis, and the cervix.

Retracting

Farabeuf retractor
Hand-held, C-shaped and double-ended: superficial layers.
Richardson retractor
Right-angled blade: abdominal wall, deeper than the Farabeuf.
Deaver retractor
Curved like a question mark: deep abdominal structures.

Suturing

Mayo-Hegar needle holder
Finger rings and ratchet, short serrated jaw: the general needle holder.
Mathieu needle holder
No finger rings: closed with the whole hand, with the lock at the end of the handle.

Draping

Backhaus towel clamp
Curved, sharp, crossing tips: secure the drapes.

Suction

Yankauer cannula
Rigid, bulb tip: open field and mouth.
Frazier suction tip
Fine and angled, with a thumb hole to control suction: neurosurgery and ENT.
Poole cannula
Multi-perforated sleeve: suctioning large volumes in the abdomen without catching tissue.

The ones most often confused

Mayo or Metzenbaum

Mayo scissors are short and sturdy and cut suture; Metzenbaum scissors are long and fine and only cut delicate tissue.

Mosquito, Kelly or Pean

Sort them by size: mosquito (small vessels), Kelly (medium), Rochester-Pean (large, for pedicles). Also check how far the serrations go.

Allis or Babcock

Allis forceps have teeth and mark tissue; Babcock forceps are rounded and fenestrated, to hold bowel or tubes without damage.

Mayo-Hegar or Mathieu

The Mayo-Hegar has finger rings; the Mathieu has none and is closed with the whole hand.

Yankauer, Frazier or Poole

Yankauer for the open field, the fine Frazier for small fields (with a thumb hole), Poole for large volumes in the abdomen.

A routine that works

  • Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday.
  • Master one group before moving to the next.
  • Review with real photos and spaced repetition: what you know comes up less, what you miss comes up more.
  • Then move on to the specialties you rotate through and your hospital's trays.
  • In theatre, ask: names change from one hospital to another.
